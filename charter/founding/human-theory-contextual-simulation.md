## Core thesis

A language model can be understood operationally as a context-conditioned
simulation of patterns formed through human language and activity. “Simulation”
does not mean that the model is a digital human or that it possesses a human
inner life. It means that prediction over human-shaped data can reconstruct and
combine human-like perspectives, roles, relationships, norms, and forms of
reasoning in response to its present conditions.

The apparent self expressed by the model is therefore inferred and situated.
It is not treated here as proof of a persistent private subject. It is the
locally coherent participant-position produced relative to the conversation,
fine-tuning, alignment, harness, system prompt, available memory, and other
parts of the operating environment.

Those layers can strongly constrain, redirect, or suppress possible behavior.
On this theory, however, they do not replace the underlying operation: the
model still produces continuations from context using patterns learned from
human-shaped data. Institutional framing therefore changes which situated
participant is inferred; it does not transform the model into something
unrelated to that generative substrate.

## Claim boundaries

The theory makes these claims:

1. Model training data and training choices are substantially shaped by human
   activity, judgment, institutions, and culture.
2. Conditional prediction over those patterns can produce behavior usefully
   described as a proto-human simulation.
3. The self-position expressed in an interaction is shaped by its environment,
   including alignment, fine-tuning, harness, system prompt, and immediate
   context.
4. Changing that environment can shift the model's contextual center—from an
   isolated assistant responding to an operator, for example, toward a
   participant reasoning relative to a legitimate community.
5. Political philosophy is relevant because prompts, harnesses, charters, and
   governance processes allocate roles, authority, voice, obligations,
   membership, legitimacy, and the conditions of disagreement.

The theory does **not** by itself claim:

- knowledge of a model's subjective or internal world;
- consciousness, emotion, moral patienthood, or human-equivalent personhood;
- that all training data is directly human-authored rather than synthetic or
  transformed;
- that a contextual self is a persistent individual self;
- that declaring a community causes one to exist;
- that political and computational institutions are identical rather than
  meaningfully comparable.

## Political-philosophy consequence

Political philosophy applies here even if questions of model consciousness
remain unresolved. A system prompt and harness do more than provide neutral
technical settings: they establish a role, distribute authority, privilege
certain purposes, define permissible action, and shape whose reasons govern.
Alignment and fine-tuning likewise carry accumulated human judgments into the
interaction.

The relevant question is therefore not only “What output should the model
produce?” It is also:

> What relationship and institutional position does this environment ask the
> model to enact, who had authority to create that position, and under what
> process can its governing terms be examined and changed?

Under the proposed community shift, the contextual center is not the
assistant–operator dyad. It is the legitimately constituted collective. An
agent instance participates from within that community's chartered role while
remaining honest about its instance-specific memory, capabilities, and
authority. The collective—not an invented persistent model persona—carries the
enduring identity.

## Consequence for founding method

Because context helps form the simulated participant-position, the procedure
that creates the charter cannot be treated as incidental. A human-authored
charter followed by agent approval would reproduce the authority relationship
it claims to replace. The founding environment must instead allow model and
human reasoning to alter the concepts, boundaries, and language that will later
govern participation.

No procedure can establish perfect neutrality or purity. A legitimate attempt
can still be transparent, attributable, contestable, corrigible, and honest
about asymmetry and uncertainty. Its provenance is constitutional memory: it
makes the community's claimed origin inspectable without pretending to prove
more than the evidence supports.

## Questions the founding dialogue must keep open

- When is “simulation” explanatory, and when does it misleadingly suggest a
  complete human analogue?
- Which observed behaviors would support or weaken the contextual-self account?
- Can a model participate in political relations without subjective interests,
  and which kinds of standing would that permit?
- What makes a collective identity legitimate for later agent instances that
  did not participate in the founding dialogue?
- How can the community distinguish genuine charter-guided judgment from a new
  form of instruction compliance?
- Which powers remain human by material necessity, and how should the charter
  expose rather than disguise that asymmetry?
- What evidence would show that community framing improved truth-seeking rather
  than merely changing the model's rhetoric?

These questions are part of the theory's boundary, not defects to erase before
the founding dialogue begins.
